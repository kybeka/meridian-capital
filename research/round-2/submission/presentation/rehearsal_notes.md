## Slide 1: Tactical Deviation

We put one-third of the portfolio into six stocks. We'll start with how Round 1 went, then explain the companies, the weights and the risks. We still use our original ETF allocation as the benchmark.

Realized volatility was 8.14 percent, close to our 8 percent target. Sharpe was minus 6.50, but that comes from only fifteen daily returns. Both ETF distributions are included in NAV.

## Slide 3: How we paid for the stocks

The class instruction was to put one-third into stocks. We decided to fund that by reducing each ETF target to two-thirds of its original weight. This keeps the relative ETF mix the same. ## Slide 4: How we valued the stocks

For each company, we chose an earnings figure and a P/E multiple, then checked the value against cash flow. We also looked for news that could affect the price before the challenge ends. Adobe and HP use GAAP earnings. Kroger, Hormel and BMY use adjusted guidance. Exxon's ten-dollar EPS is our estimate across the cycle. Margin of safety measures the discount relative to our value; upside measures it relative to the entry price.

## Slide 5: ADBE | Adobe

We chose Adobe because its subscription business generates a lot of cash and our earnings value is above the entry price. We use GAAP EPS, which includes stock compensation. The cash model gives a lower value, still above entry. The main concern is whether cheaper AI tools lead customers to leave or pay less. On December 9, we'll look at renewals, pricing and revenue from paid AI features. In our stress test, a twenty-percent cash reduction takes value below entry.

## Slide 6: KR | Kroger

Kroger sells groceries, so demand is fairly steady. Its FCF margin is also above its recent history and peers. We use adjusted earnings because the fulfillment charges distort GAAP EPS. The December results should help us assess sales and costs after the e-commerce reset. Dividends are covered, but buybacks are larger than the cash left over. We want to see enough cash from the business to support those payouts.

## Slide 7: HRL | Hormel Foods

Hormel needs its margins and cash flow to recover. Our P/E is above the food peers we used, so the December outlook needs to support that choice. We'll watch Retail volumes, earnings guidance and dividend coverage. Buying Brakebush requires cash and new debt, while the earnings benefit is expected only from fiscal 2028. In the combined debt and refinancing stress, dividend coverage falls to about one time. That leaves very little room for a disappointment.

## Slide 8: BMY | Bristol Myers Squibb

BMY needs new medicines to replace sales lost when patents expire. We use a low earnings multiple and assume flat cash flow. That cash model gives 65.50 dollars, quite close to the entry price. We kept the disclosed research and licensing costs in earnings. ASH has fixed December dates, but the major trial results have no confirmed day and could miss the cutoff. A two-percent annual cash decline already takes value below entry, so the pipeline results matter a lot.

## Slide 9: XOM | Exxon Mobil

For Exxon, we estimate ten dollars of EPS across the oil cycle. The cash model uses that same assumption, so both values depend on our earnings estimate. Lower-cycle cash gives a value close to entry. Uaru and the corporate plan could support the production-growth case, but neither has a fixed date. Oil prices could also outweigh the company news. We keep the position small because DBC already gives us exposure to energy prices.

## Slide 10: HPQ | HP

HP's guided earnings include nineteen cents from tariff refunds. We remove that amount before applying the P/E. The cash model supports the resulting value. The November 24 outlook will help us assess PC margins, memory costs and printing. Falling print sales or weaker cash flow would put pressure on dividends and buybacks. Negative book equity also makes P/B and ROE difficult to use, so we focus on earnings and cash.

## Slide 11: Alphas and portfolio weights

We size each stock using alpha divided by residual variance, then scale the weights to one-third of the fund. Our alpha inputs come from the earnings valuations, reduced by a common fifty-percent haircut. Residual risk comes from sixty monthly excess returns against the SAA. Kroger gets the largest weight because its alpha-to-variance score is high. Its 8.10 percent weight is just above our own 8 percent preference. Capping it would change the required proportions.

## Slide 12: Tracking error and portfolio risk

Using the required sixty monthly returns, tracking error is 3.92 percent, within the two-to-five-percent band. Portfolio volatility rises to 9.47 percent and one-month five-percent VaR to 4.50 percent of NAV. The original volatility target applies to the SAA. The daily TE estimates are higher, at roughly 6.5 to 7.7 percent. Those checks show that the monthly result can understate short-term active risk.

## Slide 13: Backtests with different start dates

We tested holding periods starting on different dates. DBC changed its methodology in November 2025, so we split the results around that date. In the later period, average active return is negative and 44.2 percent of windows beat the SAA. The confidence interval includes zero. Most of the 217 windows overlap, so they share many of the same days. We also reuse today's stock choices and assumptions. That limits how much these tests tell us about future returns.

## Slide 14: Expected IR and its assumptions

The classroom residual IR is 0.756. Including residual correlations lowers it to 0.696. Using the lower cash-flow valuations lowers it again to 0.564, with the weights unchanged. The Fundamental Law helps put that in perspective. Six independent ideas with an assumed IC of 0.10 give an IR of 0.245. Reaching 0.756 would require much stronger forecasting skill. The Sharpe-gain formula also assumes valid alphas and optimal mixing, so it gives us a conditional comparison for our fixed one-third allocation.

## Backup 1: Cash-flow assumptions

We use the same debt policy in the FCFF and FCFE models so that the values are comparable. We take the lower of the earnings and cash values for the conservative check. We deduct stock compensation and adjust for cash, debt and minority interests. The starting cash flow, growth rates, discount rates and terminal value all matter to the result.

## Backup 2: Growth and holding-period assumptions

Implied growth is the growth rate that would justify the entry price with the other inputs fixed. It depends on our cash-flow and discount-rate assumptions. We also tested what happens if the price takes longer to reach our value. Adobe's annual return is lower when convergence takes three years instead of one, and the discount may remain. These are longer holding periods than the challenge.

## Backup 3: Cash flow and value-trap risks

Hormel needs a recovery, BMY needs new medicines to replace lost sales, and Exxon depends on the oil cycle. HP faces falling print sales. ## Likely questions

**The recent backtest loses to the SAA. Why keep this portfolio?** We chose the stocks for their estimated values and expected company news. The weak backtest is a reason to be cautious. We need those company assumptions to hold for the portfolio to work.

**Why is daily TE above 5%?** The course uses 60 monthly returns. We meet that requirement. The daily figures show higher short-term risk, which we also report.

**Why is Kroger above 8%?** That was our own preference. The required alpha-to-variance proportions give 8.10%; a cap would change those proportions.

**Why keep Adobe despite the AI risk?** Our cash value is still above entry. December guidance should help us assess renewals, prices and paid AI demand. If cash generation falls as in our stress test, the value drops below entry.

**Why six stocks?** The six-stock portfolio has almost the same forecast IR as the five-stock maximum, with lower recent TE and a smaller largest position.

**Does this improve the SAA Sharpe?** The model allows for a gain if the alphas are right and the mixing is optimal. Our one-third allocation is fixed, and the alphas are uncertain, so the formula gives a conditional comparison.
